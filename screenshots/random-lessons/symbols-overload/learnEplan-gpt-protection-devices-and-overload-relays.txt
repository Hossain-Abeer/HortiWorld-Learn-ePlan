1. WHY THE SYMBOL CATEGORIES MATTER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In EPLAN, symbols are grouped by the **function** they perform in the
circuit[reference:0]. The folder names tell you what the component is
designed to do:

- **"Protection device" (FA/FT series):** These are components whose
  *primary* job is to protect the circuit. They detect abnormal
  conditions (overload, short circuit) and act to prevent damage.
  Examples: Circuit breakers (FA), thermal overload relays (FT), fuses.
  They are connected directly in the main power path (R, Y, B).

- **"Protective circuit" (coils, contacts):** These are components
  that *control* the circuit. They execute commands to open or close
  the power path during normal operation.
  Examples: Contactor coils (K), NO/NC contacts, pushbuttons.
  The contactor (K1) is a switching device; it does not protect itself
  or the motor from overloads[reference:1].

**Rule of thumb:** Protection devices *sense and protect*. Protective
circuit components *switch and control*.


2. WHY USE A THERMAL OVERLOAD RELAY (OLR) WHEN YOU HAVE A BREAKER (MCB)?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
They protect against **two different types of faults**:

- **Circuit Breaker (MCB - Q1):** Protects against **short circuits**
  and severe overloads. It trips instantly (magnetic) or very fast
  (thermal) when current is dangerously high (e.g., thousands of amps).
  It protects the *wiring* and prevents fire.

- **Thermal Overload Relay (OLR - F1/FT):** Protects the **motor** from
  **long-term, low-level overloads** (e.g., a jammed conveyor belt).
  It uses bimetallic strips that bend slowly as current heats them,
  simulating how the motor itself heats up[reference:2].
  It trips after seconds or minutes, not instantly.

**Why the MCB alone is not enough:** A motor can draw 5-7 times its
rated current during normal startup. An MCB sized for the motor might
trip during startup (nuisance tripping). An OLR has a **time delay**
that tolerates startup current but still trips if the overload persists.
The MCB protects against *short circuits*; the OLR protects against
*thermal overloads*.


3. WHY IS THE OLR POSITIONED AFTER THE CONTACTOR?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The standard order is: **MCB → Contactor (K1) → OLR → Motor**[reference:3].

**Reason 1: The OLR signals the contactor to trip.**
The OLR does not have the power to cut the main circuit itself.
It has an auxiliary NC contact (95-96) that is wired into the
contactor coil circuit. When the OLR detects an overload, it opens
this NC contact, which de-energizes the contactor coil, and the
contactor's main contacts open to disconnect the motor[reference:4].
The OLR *detects*; the contactor *acts*.

**Reason 2: Physical and thermal design.**
Most industrial OLRs (e.g., Schneider LRD series) are
mechanically designed to bolt directly onto the bottom of the
contactor[reference:5]. This saves panel space and simplifies
wiring. Placing the OLR after the contactor also means the
contactor is not exposed to the heat generated by the OLR's
bimetallic elements during an overload[reference:6].

**Reason 3: Protection coverage.**
When the OLR is after the contactor, it monitors the current that
actually flows to the motor. If the contactor's contacts degrade
and cause a slight increase in resistance, the OLR (if after the
contactor) would still see the true motor current. If placed
before, a faulty contactor could mask a motor overload.

**In summary:**
MCB protects against short circuits → Contactor switches the motor
on/off → OLR protects the motor from prolonged overloads and
triggers the contactor to trip.
